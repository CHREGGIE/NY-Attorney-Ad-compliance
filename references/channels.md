## 1. Website: homepage, practice-area pages, About
- Responsible firm name and phone or office address appear sitewide (usually the footer). Check that standalone landing pages, popups, and PDFs carry them too.
- Hero copy: superlatives, outcome language, unsupported "specialist" or "expert" claims.
- Practice-area pages: legal statements must be accurate for **New York** (generic AI or national content often states other states' law). Deadlines and statutes of limitation must be correct and current.
- "Offices" match real locations. Map pins and addresses match the GBP.
- Firm name matches the entity name (PLLC/LLP/PC) in at least the footer or legal notice.
- No "Attorney Advertising" requirement. If present, it's harmless. Recommending removal is a style choice, not a compliance fix.
- A website disclaimer (no attorney-client relationship formed by contact or use of the site, don't send confidential information) isn't required by 7.1, but it's good practice because of Rule 1.18 (prospective clients). Note it if missing; don't Block.

## 2. Attorney bios
- Admissions with years; courts admitted; education; publications; bar roles. All verifiable.
- Years of experience anchored to a year, not a number that goes stale.
- Representative matters: accurate, consent-checked or anonymized, not overstated (e.g., "led" vs. "was on the team").
- Prior roles (former prosecutor, clerk, in-house) stated as fact, with no influence implication.
- Awards with issuer and year. Certifications with the certifying body named.
- Titles accurate: partner vs. counsel vs. associate (7.5 Cmt [5]).
- Personal details fine. Avoid anything that implies results.

## 3. Case results / representative matters pages
- Each result: matter type, year, forum, outcome, and any material post-result event (appeal, reduction, uncollected).
- Recommend (not require) the "Prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome" disclaimer on the page and near any featured result.
- No cherry-picked "win rates".
- Client consent or anonymization for each matter (1.6).
- Remove results that were reversed or vacated, or label them accurately.

## 4. Blog and legal content (incl. GEO/AI-search content)
- Accuracy of law for NY and the date; add "last reviewed" dates on evergreen legal guides.
- Statistics sourced.
- Calls to action at the end are marketing: apply the red-flag scan.
- Content written to be quoted by AI answers (FAQ blocks, "What is…" definitions, comparison tables) is still a communication about the firm's services wherever it describes the firm. "Ainsworth Gorkin is the leading…" in an FAQ answer is the same superlative problem, now amplified by AI citation.
- Don't write answers that read as individualized legal advice to a specific reader's matter.

## 5. Google Business Profile
- **Business name:** exactly the real-world name. No keywords, locations, or taglines (Google policy; also 7.5).
- **Categories and services:** accurate practice areas only.
- **Description (750 chars):** red-flag scan; no URLs or promo codes (Google policy).
- **Posts and offers:** red-flag scan; attribution is satisfied by the profile itself.
- **Photos:** real team and office photos. Stock images implying a bigger office or team mislead.
- **Review requests:** ask all clients the same way (no gating), no incentives, no employee reviews.
- **Review replies:**
  - Never confirm the reviewer was a client, and never discuss matter facts, outcomes, or fees (1.6). This applies to negative reviews especially. Rebutting with case facts is a classic violation. NY permits limited disclosure only to defend against accusations of wrongful conduct, and only to the extent reasonably necessary (1.6(b)(5)(i)). A public review reply rarely qualifies.
  - Good positive reply: thanks, no details. "Thank you for the kind words. We appreciate you taking the time." Good negative reply: no details, an invitation offline. "We take all feedback seriously. Because of our professional obligations we can't discuss any matter here. Please contact [partner] at [phone]."
- **Q&A:** answers must be accurate and generic, not individualized advice.

## 6. Directories (Avvo, Justia, Martindale, Super Lawyers, FindLaw, bar listings)
- Name, address, and phone consistent with the website and GBP.
- Practice areas accurate. Directories often auto-populate categories the firm doesn't handle; remove them.
- "Specialist" labels some directories apply automatically: check they're accurate and that no "certified" language appears.
- Ratings presented as ratings, not certifications.
- Paid "featured" placement is advertising (fine) but shouldn't be presented on the firm's site as an award.

## 7. Email, newsletters, client alerts
- Rule 7.1 now covers emails to existing clients and other lawyers.
- Footer: responsible lawyer or firm name + phone or address (7.1(d)). CAN-SPAM requires a physical postal address and an opt-out for commercial email anyway.
- No "ATTORNEY ADVERTISING" subject-line requirement anymore.
- Targeted emails to a specific person known to need services in a particular matter are written solicitation: allowed under 7.3, but subject to 7.1 and 7.3(c) (stop if they asked not to be contacted).
- Client alerts: legal accuracy plus the general red-flag scan on any service pitch.

## 8. LinkedIn and social media
- Profile headlines and About sections: red-flag scan; "specialist" vs. "certified".
- LinkedIn "Skills" endorsements aren't certifications. Don't repost them as credentials.
- Posts celebrating wins: same rules as case results (context, consent, disclaimer recommended).
- DMs to prospects: written solicitation analysis (see §12).
- Graphics and short videos: 7.1(d) attribution, either through the profile itself or on standalone ads.
- Commenting on others' legal problems in public threads with "DM me, I can help" is directed to a specific person with a known matter. It's written, not live, so it's outside 7.3(b), but watch for 7.3(c) and tone (coercion or pressure).

## 9. Video, podcasts, webinars
- Actors or dramatizations: no longer a specific rule, but undisclosed dramatizations presented as real clients or real events mislead (7.1(a)). Recommend on-screen disclosure.
- Spoken claims get the same red-flag scan as text.
- Attribution in the description, end card, or channel.
- Webinars: educational content is fine. Avoid individualized advice to attendees about their matters in public Q&A.

## 10. Paid ads (search, social, display, Google Local Services Ads)
- Headline character limits push toward superlatives ("Top NYC Lawyers"). Rewrite to specifics ("NYC Commercial Litigation Since [year]").
- Ad extensions (callouts, sitelinks, structured snippets): red-flag scan each.
- Landing page must deliver what the ad claims.
- Google LSA "Google Screened" badge: describe it accurately; it's not a certification.
- Competitor-name keyword bidding: allowed by Google. Ad copy that implies affiliation with the competitor is misleading (7.1(a)).

## 11. Schema markup and hidden content
- The old ban on meta tags or hidden code "that would violate these Rules if displayed" was removed, but hidden content is still a communication. Hidden misleading claims violate 7.1(a).
- Meta titles and descriptions show in search results: red-flag scan ("Best NYC Lawyers | Firm").
- JSON-LD: LegalService/Attorney `name` must match the real name; `aggregateRating` or `review` on your own entity is self-serving and isn't eligible for Google review rich results. Flag it as Revise, or Block if the ratings are invented. `award`, `knowsAbout`, and `hasCredential` values get the same scrutiny as visible copy.
- Image alt text: no keyword-stuffed superlatives.

## 12. Outreach and business-development scripts
Decision path:
1. Is it directed to a **specific person** the firm knows (or should know) **needs legal services in a particular matter**? If no, it's not solicitation; apply 7.1 only.
2. If yes, is it **live person-to-person** (in person, live phone, live video)? If no (email, letter, DM), it's allowed, subject to 7.1 and 7.3(c).
3. If live: is the person (1) a lawyer, (2) someone with a family, close personal, or prior business or professional relationship with the firm, or (3) someone who **routinely uses this type of legal service for business purposes**? If yes, it's allowed. If no, **Block**.
4. In all cases: stop on request (7.3(c)(1)); no coercion, duress, or harassment (7.3(c)(2)).
5. **Is someone other than the firm's lawyers doing the soliciting** (appointment setters, callers, lead runners, "business development reps")? Flag Judiciary Law §§479/482 for attorney review, regardless of the ethics-rule analysis.

Examples:
- Calling the GC of a company the firm met at a conference about a contract dispute the GC mentioned: allowed (prior professional relationship, and a routine user of legal services).
- Cold-calling individual defendants named in newly filed lawsuits: Block (live, specific matter, no relationship, likely not routine users).
- Cold-calling owners of mid-size companies just named as defendants in commercial suits: case-by-case under 7.3(b)(3). Does this owner routinely hire litigation counsel for the business? Written outreach is the safer default.
- Emailing those same defendants a short, accurate note: allowed (written), subject to 7.1 and 7.3(c).

## 13. Referral, partnership, and sponsorship programs
- Anything of value tied to referrals or to a recommendation resulting in a client: 7.2(a) Block.
- Fee-sharing with other lawyers only under 1.5(g).
- Reciprocal referral arrangements with nonlawyer professionals: Rule 5.8 (formal contractual arrangements have specific requirements). Non-exclusive informal reciprocal referrals are outside 5.8 but still can't involve payment.
- Pay-per-lead or pay-per-case marketing vendors: escalate for attorney review (7.2(a), Judiciary Law §482). Pure advertising (pay-per-click, flat directory fee) is generally fine.
- Sponsorships and charity: fine. Don't condition them on referrals.
- Paying press for publicity in a news item: the old explicit ban was removed, but undisclosed paid placement presented as independent news is misleading (7.1(a)) and may violate FTC endorsement guidance.

## 14. Firm names, trade names, domains, handles, phone vanity numbers
- Must not be false, deceptive, or misleading (7.5(b)(1)).
- No "legal aid", "legal services office", "legal assistance office", or "defender office" terms for a private firm (7.5(b)(2)(i)). No "non-profit"/"not-for-profit" (ii). No nonlawyer names (iii). PC/LLC/LLP designators in the entity name (iv)–(v).
- Trade names (acronyms, street names) are allowed if not misleading (Op. 1207). Watch names that imply a government connection ("NY State Business Court Lawyers"), an outcome ("WinMyCase Law"), or a size or geography the firm doesn't have.
- Vanity phone numbers with a motto: allowed if the motto doesn't otherwise violate the rules (7.5(b)(2)(vi)). "1-800-WIN-BIG" implies results: Block.
- Retired or deceased partner names only with a continuing line of succession (7.5 Cmt [2]–[3]).
- Lawyers holding public office: not in the firm name during any substantial period of inactive practice (7.5 Cmt [4]).
