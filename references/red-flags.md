# Red-flag pattern library

Each pattern lists why it's risky, its default severity, and compliant rewrites. Context can move the severity up or down. A superlative backed by an objective, cited fact may be fine. A "true" result with misleading omissions may be a Block.

### 1. Superlatives and quality claims
**Triggers:** best, top, leading, premier, #1, elite, finest, unmatched, unparalleled, second to none, most trusted / respected / successful / experienced, "the firm to call".
**Why:** They can't be verified and they imply comparison. Under the ABA-derived comments, unsubstantiated characterizations can be misleading.
**Default:** Revise. Escalate to Block if paired with a factual falsehood.
**Rewrite pattern:** Replace the adjective with a verifiable fact.
- "NYC's top commercial litigators" → "Commercial litigators in New York state and federal courts since [year]"
- "Unmatched trial experience" → "[N] commercial matters taken through trial since [year]"
- "The most trusted name in business disputes" → "Business disputes are the core of our practice"

Self-descriptions of style ("we're responsive", "we communicate clearly") are generally fine if true. Prefer specifics like "a partner returns calls within one business day", but only if the firm actually does it.

### 2. Outcome guarantees and results promises
**Triggers:** guarantee, we win, we always win, never lose, results guaranteed, we'll get you, "get you the compensation you deserve", maximum recovery, "we get results", 100%.
**Why:** It promises outcomes no lawyer controls, and it creates unjustified expectations.
**Default:** Block ("guarantee", "never lose"). Revise for softer outcome language.
**Rewrite:** Describe the process and commitment, not the outcome.
- "We win" → "We prepare every case as if it's going to trial"
- "We'll get you what you deserve" → "We'll assess your claim and tell you plainly what it's worth pursuing"

### 3. Case results, verdicts, settlements, win rates
**Triggers:** $ amounts, "verdict", "settlement", "recovered", "won", "% success", "win rate", "dismissed".
**Why:** Literally true results can mislead without context: whether the result was reduced or reversed on appeal or still pending, what portion was collected, the facts that drove it, and how representative it is. Win rates depend on case selection and are almost never apples-to-apples.
**Default:** Revise (results without context). Block (false, superseded, or reversed results presented as final; a win rate without a clear basis).
**Rewrite:** Matter type + year + forum + outcome + voluntary disclaimer.
- "$4.2M verdict!" → "$4.2M jury verdict for a minority shareholder, [Court], [year]. Prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome."
- "98% success rate" → remove, or restate as a factual count with a defined basis: "[N] of [M] arbitrations resolved in the client's favor, [years], as defined by [basis]". Consider removing it entirely.
**Also check:** client consent (1.6) for identifiable matters; whether the result is public record (still not automatically "generally known").

### 4. Comparisons with other lawyers
**Triggers:** "more than any other firm", "better than", "unlike other firms", "the only firm that…", "bigger", "faster".
**Why:** They need factual substantiation as of the publication date, and they're rarely provable.
**Default:** Revise. Block if demonstrably false.
**Rewrite:** Drop the comparison and keep the underlying fact. "Unlike big firms, a partner handles your case" → "A partner handles every matter from intake to resolution", if true.

### 5. Certified / specialist / expert
**Triggers:** certified, board certified, certification, specialist, specializes, specialty, expert, expertise.
**Rule:** 7.1(c) applies only to **certified**. The certifying organization must be approved or ABA-accredited **and** named in the communication. "Specialize", "specialist", and "concentrate" are allowed if accurate (Cmt [7]). "Expert" isn't defined. Treat it like "specialist": fine if true, risky if it implies a credential.
**Default:** Block (certified without a named certifier). Verify ("specialist" or "expert").
**Rewrite:** "Board-certified litigator" → "Certified in Civil Trial Law by the National Board of Trial Advocacy" (only if true and the body qualifies), or "Our practice focuses on commercial litigation".

### 6. Awards, ratings, rankings
**Triggers:** Super Lawyers, Rising Stars, Best Lawyers, Martindale AV Preeminent, Avvo 10.0, Chambers, Legal 500, "Top 100", "Top 10", "Lawyer of the Year", badges.
**Why:** Awards are fine if bona fide and accurately described. They mislead when the issuer or year is missing, when they're expired, when they're presented as certifications, or when they come from pay-to-play "awards" presented as independent honors.
**Default:** Verify (issuer, year, scope). Revise if the year or issuer is missing. Block if fabricated or expired-but-presented-as-current.
**Rewrite:** "Super Lawyer" → "Selected to Super Lawyers®, New York Metro, [year]". Check the issuer's own usage rules too. Many require specific wording.

### 7. Experience and size claims
**Triggers:** "over X years", "decades of combined experience", "hundreds of cases", "thousands of clients", attorney counts, office counts.
**Why:** They go stale (copy written in 2019 still says "15 years"). "Combined experience" inflates the impression of a small firm's depth.
**Default:** Verify. Revise "combined experience" phrasing.
**Rewrite:** Anchor to a year so it never goes stale: "Practicing since [year]" or "Founded in [year]". Use an individual's experience instead of a combined total.

### 8. Implied partnership, affiliation, or structure
**Triggers:** "& Associates" (with no associates), "our team of litigators" (two lawyers), "partners" for non-partners, "of counsel" or "affiliate" relationships, "offices in" (virtual addresses or shared-space mail drops), "nationwide", "network of firms".
**Rule:** 7.1(a), 7.5(b)(1)(iii), 7.5 Cmt [5].
**Default:** Revise. Block if the structure is false (claiming a partnership or affiliation that doesn't exist).
**Rewrite:** Describe the actual structure. "Our team" is fine if it accurately covers lawyers plus staff. "Offices in Manhattan and Brooklyn" only if both are real offices. Otherwise say "meetings available in Brooklyn by appointment".

### 9. Influence and insider claims
**Triggers:** "relationships with judges", "we know the prosecutors", "former judge knows how they think", "inside track", "connections at [agency]".
**Why:** Implying an ability to improperly influence a court, agency, or official is misleading per se and touches 8.4. A former role ("former Assistant District Attorney") stated as plain biography is generally fine.
**Default:** Block (influence implication). Fine (neutral biographical fact).
**Rewrite:** "She knows how DAs think" → "She served as an Assistant District Attorney in [County] from [year] to [year]".

### 10. Testimonials and reviews
**Triggers:** client quotes, star ratings, "5-star", review widgets, video testimonials, review request emails.
**Why:** The old NY testimonial rules (no testimonials about pending matters, disclose paid endorsements) were eliminated, but misleading testimonials still violate 7.1(a). The FTC rule bans fake, AI-generated, bought, or insider reviews and review suppression. Google bans incentives and review gating.
**Risk checks:** Is the testimonial real and unedited in substance? Is the matter still pending (a testimonial about a matter that could still go wrong may mislead)? Was the reviewer compensated (disclose it)? Does the testimonial imply results the firm can't promise? Does publishing it reveal confidential information?
**Default:** Block (fabricated, bought, AI-written, or gated). Verify (consent, accuracy). Note (add context).

### 11. Client names, logos, case studies
**Triggers:** "clients include", logo walls, named-matter case studies, "we represented [Company] in…".
**Rule:** 1.6. The fact of the representation can itself be confidential. Get informed written consent. Public filings don't automatically make it "generally known".
**Default:** Verify (consent on file?). Block (sensitive matters, or consent known to be absent).
**Rewrite:** Anonymize the matter: "Represented a [industry] distributor in a $[X]M breach-of-contract dispute against a former supplier", plus check that the description isn't identifiable.

### 12. Fees and "no fee unless" claims
**Triggers:** "free consultation", "no fee unless we win", "no recovery, no fee", flat fees, hourly rates, "affordable", "low cost".
**Why:** The old fee-advertising mechanics are gone, but fee claims must be accurate and complete. "No fee unless we win" is misleading if the client is still liable for costs and disbursements. Advertised flat fees must match what's actually charged for that scope.
**Default:** Revise (incomplete "no fee" claims). Verify (stated rates and fees). "Free consultation" is fine if true.
**Rewrite:** "No fee unless we win" → "No attorney's fee unless we recover for you. [Costs and expenses: explain who pays.]"

### 13. Urgency, fear, and document look-alikes
**Triggers:** "act now or lose your rights", countdown timers, "URGENT LEGAL NOTICE", mailers styled like court papers or summonses, government-style seals.
**Why:** A look-alike is likely misleading under 7.1 and deceptive under 8.4(c) (NYSBA). Overstated deadline fear can misrepresent the law.
**Default:** Block (document or government look-alike). Revise (overstated urgency). Accurate statute-of-limitations info stated neutrally is fine.

### 14. Referral incentives
**Triggers:** referral bonus, gift card for referrals, "refer a friend", referral reward, commission, "finder's fee", pay-per-lead, pay-per-signed-case.
**Rule:** 7.2(a). Also Judiciary Law §482 if someone is paid to solicit.
**Default:** Block (value given for recommendations). Paying for advertising or listings is fine. Thank-you notes not tied to value are fine.
**Rewrite:** Swap rewards for relationship-building that isn't conditioned on referrals: a client appreciation event, a useful newsletter, a handwritten thank-you.

### 15. AI-drafted content artifacts
**Triggers:** invented statistics ("90% of business disputes settle…" with no source), fabricated case citations, fake awards, generic "seasoned attorneys with decades of experience", placeholders like [Firm Name], "lorem ipsum", "As an AI".
**Why:** Fabricated facts are false statements (7.1(a), 8.4(c)). Generic boilerplate is often inaccurate for the specific firm.
**Default:** Block (fabricated facts or citations). Revise (generic claims).
**Action:** Every statistic needs a source the firm can produce. Every legal statement needs to be correct for New York.

### 16. Missing attribution
**Rule:** 7.1(d). Name of at least one responsible lawyer or firm, plus contact info (phone or physical address per Cmt [10]).
**Where it bites:** standalone landing pages, social graphics, video ads, email templates, PDFs, directory profiles, AI-search snippets drawn from the firm's pages.
**Default:** Revise. On a website, a sitewide footer usually satisfies it. Check that standalone pages have the footer too.
